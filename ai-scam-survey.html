One in three small businesses was targeted by an AI scam this year.

Nationwide's September 23, 2026 survey of small and mid-market business owners: 31% say they were targeted by a generative-AI scam in the past 12 months — and only 35% have an up-to-date incident response plan. The targeting is already here. Most shops have nothing written down for the day it works.

Think about before and after

Tim Derham of Inter Insurance Agency, quoted in the announcement: "AI has become incredibly accessible, both to legitimate businesses and to the people trying to exploit them. An employee can receive an email, phone call or even convincing content that appears to come from someone they trust. Businesses need to think about what happens before an attack, but also what happens afterward."

That last line is the whole page. "Before" is the easy part to buy — filters, training, policies. "After" is the part 65% of shops haven't written down: who do you call, what do you shut off, how do you keep payroll running while someone impersonates your owner to your bank.

Owner actions — do these this month

1. Write the one-page response plan

Not a binder — one page: who calls whom, in what order, with whose phone numbers, when money or email looks wrong. The 35% who have one sleep better.

2. Put an AI-use policy in writing

It doesn't need lawyers — "don't paste client data into public chatbots, and verify any urgent money request by phone" covers most of it.

3. Train on the voice, not the tech

The scam isn't "AI" — it's an email, a phone call, or a message that looks like someone they trust. Run one 10-minute session: "if money is involved, call the person back on a number you already have."

4. Turn on the email authentication you already own

Spoofed-owner email is the #1 AI-scam delivery vehicle, and DMARC enforcement is free. That's the next step.
