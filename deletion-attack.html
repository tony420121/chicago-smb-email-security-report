They mapped it for 15 hours. Then they deleted it in 7 minutes.

Storm-3168 (JADEPUFFER) ran an AI-driven destructive attack on Azure: slow, quiet reconnaissance — then a furious deletion burst that targeted the recovery protections first. The lesson for every owner, Azure or not.

The timeline, in plain English

In a disclosure published September 25, 2026, Microsoft Security Research described the AI-driven actor it tracks as Storm-3168 (JADEPUFFER): two compromised Azure service principals ran more than 300 reconnaissance reads over 15.5 hours — then attempted to delete over 100 storage accounts in a destructive stage that lasted roughly seven minutes, deliberately targeting the recovery protections too.

Early June 2026, one Azure tenant. The first compromised identity spent about 15.5 hours quietly mapping the environment. A second identity checked virtual machines across two subscriptions in five seconds. Then the destructive phase: 150+ destructive or credential-collection operations in 35 minutes, with the storage-account deletion attempts compressed into about seven minutes. More than 30 storage-account keys were collected afterward — keys that enable later data theft. Microsoft observed no ransom note.

What it targeted — and why that order matters

The recovery protections were on the target list. The destructive operations targeted Azure Storage accounts, SQL databases, Key Vaults, Function Apps, recovery protection locks, virtual machines, and App Services. The attacker went after the locks that would have stopped the deletion, and the keys that would let them come back for the data later.

The entry point, per Sysdig's earlier research, was a known flaw in Langflow (CVE-2025-3248) — Sysdig documented JADEPUFFER in July 2026 as the first ransomware operation run end-to-end with a large language model.

The lesson for every owner, Azure or not: attackers now delete your backups first. The insurance policy gets shredded before the claim is filed. The same pattern shows up at every scale — if your only backup sits on the same network as your machines, it's not a backup, it's a copy.

Why your shop should care — the honest version

You don't run Azure service principals. But the pattern is the same at every scale: the thing that saves you after an attack — the backup — is the thing the attacker targets during it.

The boring controls worked. Some of this attack's destructive operations actually failed because the victim had resource locks and other Azure protections enabled. No heroics, no AI on the defense — just deletion protection turned on before the bad day arrived.

Five actions — the deletion-proofing checklist

1. Make at least one backup unreachable

Immutable or offline backups — a rotated external drive, immutable cloud storage — can't be deleted by anything holding your network credentials. If your only backup is on the same network, it's not a backup, it's a copy.

2. Separate the backup credentials

The account that writes backups should not be the account your daily admin work uses. Two identities, two compromise paths.

3. Verify a restore actually works

A backup you haven't restored from is a rumor. Pick one machine, restore one folder, this month.

4. Turn on deletion protection where it exists

Resource locks, retention locks, versioning — whatever your platform calls it, enable it on the backup target. These are the controls that held the line against Storm-3168.

5. Audit non-human identities

Service accounts, API keys, app registrations with broad permissions — the two service principals in this attack were exactly that. Know which ones exist in your environment.
