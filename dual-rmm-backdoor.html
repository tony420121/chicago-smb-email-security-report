Attackers install two backdoors, so killing one doesn't evict them.

Microsoft's September 30, 2026 disclosure: a campaign (running since July) installs the legitimate, digitally-signed MSP360 RMM via phishing lures — then uses it to deploy ScreenConnect as a second backdoor. Two independent remote-access channels. Remove one, the other stays open.

What happened

The campaign — first detected in July 2026 and disclosed by Microsoft's security research team on September 30, 2026 (reported October 1 by LavX News and Brussels Morning) — works in two stages. First, phishing lures (meeting invitations, PDF readers, software updates) install MSP360 RMM (v2.5.0.67) — a legitimate, digitally-signed remote-management tool. Then the attackers use MSP360's own PowerShell execution capability to deploy ConnectWise ScreenConnect as a second backdoor.

The checkable indicators: services named RMM.Agent.exe and RMM.Agent.Launcher.exe, plus a firewall rule opening port 48678 UDP. The persistence survives a restart — this is redundant remote administration by design.

Why your shop should care — the honest version

The redundancy is the attack. One backdoor gets found — by you, your IT person, or antivirus. The second keeps running. Incident response that declares "we removed it" after killing one channel leaves the other live. Killing one backdoor doesn't evict them. That's the sentence to remember — and it's strictly the persistence beat, distinct from our CSuite session-theft page and the ransomware playbook.

And the framing has to be honest, or this page misleads: MSP360 and ScreenConnect are legitimate tools doing legitimate work on thousands of networks — possibly yours, under your MSP's management. This is not "your MSP's tools are malware." The tools aren't the problem; the unapproved instances are. Don't read this as a vendor to fear — read it as an incident-response rule to learn: when you find one remote-access channel you didn't install, assume there are two until you've proven otherwise.

Three checks — do these this month

1. Hunt for unapproved RMM services

Check your machines for remote-management services you didn't approve — RMM.Agent.exe and RMM.Agent.Launcher.exe are the named ones. Ask your IT provider which RMM tools should be on your machines, then compare. The legitimate one stays; the unknown one goes.

2. Check the firewall

Look for a rule opening port 48678 UDP. If your shop has no legitimate tool using that port, it's a finding — ask your IT person to confirm what put it there.

3. Assume two, not one

If you (or your IT person) ever find an unapproved remote-access tool, the response is now: hunt the second channel before declaring the incident closed. Write that rule down now, while nothing is on fire.
