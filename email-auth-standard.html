A small-business security standard now says DMARC is mandatory — not optional.

SMB1001:2026 requires email authentication — DMARC, SPF, and DKIM — as formal controls. Honest line up front: it's not U.S. law. But "a published standard now requires it" is the strongest plain-English reason to finally fix your email.

What the standard actually says

The 2026 edition of SMB1001, the small-business cybersecurity standard from Dynamic Standards International, now mandates email authentication — DMARC, SPF, and DKIM — as formal controls against email fraud, phishing, and brand impersonation. DMARC (with SPF and DKIM) is mandatory from the Silver tier up, and the DMARC policy must be set to quarantine or reject — not the soft "none" setting that provides no protection. The same update adds Endpoint Detection and Response controls, moves cyber-awareness training into the Bronze entry-level tier, and requires a formal policy for the acceptable and secure use of AI.

Why a standard bothers mandating this

Because email compromise and identity fraud top the self-reported cybercrime charts for small business — the Australian Cyber Security Centre's 2024–25 reporting put the average cost at $56,000 AUD per incident for small businesses. An attacker who can send email as you can send your customers a fake invoice, your staff a fake wire instruction, your vendors a fake change-of-bank-details notice. DMARC with enforcement is the control that makes "as you" stop working.

The unreadiness runs deep. Per Nationwide's September 23, 2026 survey, only 35% of businesses HAVE an incident-response plan — which means most small shops would be improvising the day something goes wrong. Mandates like SMB1001 exist precisely because "we'll deal with it later" keeps losing.

What it means for your shop

You don't need to pursue SMB1001 certification to use its logic. The standard is saying, in published form, what the September 2026 scan behind this report already showed in practice: most small-business domains have no meaningful email authentication. The question isn't whether your domain can be spoofed — without DMARC enforcement, it can. The question is whether you've closed the door before someone walks through it.

3. Confirm DKIM signing everywhere

DKIM signing must be enabled on all outbound email, not just your main mailbox.

4. Publish DMARC — then enforce it

Start at p=none (monitoring, so you can see who's sending as you), then move to p=quarantine or p=reject once legitimate mail passes. The standard requires enforcement, not monitoring.
