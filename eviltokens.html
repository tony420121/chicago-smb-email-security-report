Microsoft just killed an AI-powered phishing service that had hijacked 12,000 inboxes.

It ran AI "at every step of the attack chain." It died to a federal court order. Here's what it did, how it died — and the three things your shop should do about the next one.

What EvilTokens was

EvilTokens was a phishing service — a criminal operation, run like a business — that used AI at every stage of the attack. Its specialty was device-code phishing: instead of stealing your password, it tricked victims into entering a Microsoft device-auth code the attacker had generated. Once entered, the attacker held a live session token — full access to the inbox, no password needed, and your MFA never triggered because the victim did the authenticating.

How it died

Microsoft obtained court authorization from the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia and, working with Health-ISAC, Cloudflare, OpenAI, SpyCloud, Shadowserver, Coinbase, and Railway, dismantled the operation's infrastructure. That's Microsoft, a health-security information-sharing center, an AI lab, and major infrastructure companies coordinating on one criminal service — a genuinely rare win at scale.

The win
The good guys showed up in force. A court order, the platform that owned the infrastructure, the AI company whose models were being abused, and the security community — all moving together to tear down one operation. When defenders coordinate like this, the attackers lose. EvilTokens is gone; that's the story.

Why your shop should care — the honest version

The next EvilTokens is already being built. The takedown worked, but the attack pattern it used — device-code phishing with AI-generated lures — is now a proven, mass-scale template. Your business doesn't need to be a target: these services rent their tools to anyone, and small shops are the preferred victims because they're the least hardened. EvilTokens' 12,000 inboxes weren't 12,000 Fortune 500 companies.

Here's the uncomfortable part: the report's scan found 81.5% of Chicago SMB domains lack DMARC enforcement — which means once an attacker has a session token on an employee's inbox, they can send mail as your business and most receivers will deliver it. Device-code phishing gets them in the door; missing email authentication hands them your letterhead.

Three actions — do these this month

1. Block the device-code flow

Device-code authentication exists for devices that can't do a normal sign-in (smart TVs, meeting-room gear). Your staff's laptops are not those devices. In Microsoft Entra (your Microsoft 365 admin center), disable or restrict device-code sign-in for users — it's the exact door EvilTokens walked through. Five minutes in admin, for most shops.

2. Enforce phishing-resistant MFA

SMS codes and push-notification approvals can be intercepted or fatigued into submission. Move staff to passkeys, FIDO2 security keys, or number-matching authenticator approval — authentication methods where the attacker can't forward a code and win. Start with email and finance users.

3. Revoke sessions — not just passwords

When an account is compromised, changing the password does not log out the attacker's stolen session. Session tokens survive password resets. After any suspected compromise, revoke the user's active sessions in Entra/365 admin and reset the password — in that order. Write this down as your incident step zero.
