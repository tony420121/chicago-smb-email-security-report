They don't steal your password. They steal your session.

Huntress researchers showed how a small sideloaded Windows app can open the real Microsoft login window — and walk away with your login tokens even after you complete MFA. You did everything right. The login still worked. The attacker just kept the keys.

What happened, in plain English

Windows ships with a Microsoft-signed program called WWAHost.exe — the thing that runs little web apps packaged as AppX files. Huntress's research shows an attacker can sideload their own AppX package that tells WWAHost to open a genuine Microsoft login dialog from login.microsoftonline.com. You see a real login. You type your real password. You complete your real MFA. And the attacker's code captures the OAuth authorization code and the access and refresh tokens that come back — the session keys that say "this person is logged in." (hendryadrian, Sept 27, 2026)

Why MFA doesn't stop it — the honest version

MFA protects the login. This attack steals what comes after the login. Because you authenticated through the real Microsoft flow, there's nothing fake to catch — the theft happens when Microsoft hands the tokens back. In Huntress's test, the captured token carried broad Microsoft Office delegated permissions: mail, files, Teams, directory, calendar, and contacts. Your second factor fired correctly and it didn't matter.

Now the scope, honestly: the attack's prerequisite is sideloading. Windows Developer Mode must be enabled, or your shop must have an enterprise sideloading policy that allows unsigned packages. Without that, the package registration fails. That means a well-managed fleet with Developer Mode off is not in the danger zone — but any machine where a user (or a sloppy policy) turned it on is.

Four actions — do these this month

1. Kill sideloading on work machines

Developer Mode off by policy — not by user choice. No unsigned AppX packages. This closes the front door the research walked through. Check it at Settings → Privacy & Security → For Developers on every work machine, or enforce it centrally.

2. Review app-consent and sideloading settings in Intune

No unsigned packages, and no "allow all trusted apps" leftovers from a pilot that never ended. Loose consent settings are how tokens walk out behind a legitimate login.

3. Use Conditional Access where your license reaches

Token-protection and session controls are the safety net for when a token does leak. And watch the traffic: Huntress's detection note says to look for the MSAppHost/3.0 user-agent string reaching non-Microsoft destinations — that's the stolen token leaving the building. Their writeup also includes a Group Policy mitigation for the sideloading prerequisite.

4. Move admin accounts to phishing-resistant MFA

FIDO2 security keys or Windows Hello for Business for every admin. Stolen sessions bypass everything weaker — and admins' sessions are the ones that matter.
