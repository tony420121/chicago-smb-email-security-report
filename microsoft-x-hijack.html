They hijacked Microsoft’s own account.

On October 1, attackers briefly took over @Microsoft — the 13-million-follower account — swapped the logo for Clippy, and pushed a fake $Clippy crypto token. If the biggest account in your feed can push a scam, your shop's Facebook page is one reused password away.

What happened, briefly

On October 1, 2026, attackers took over @Microsoft — briefly — followed and reposted a fake @clippymsftcto account, swapped the profile picture for Clippy, and pushed a fake $Clippy crypto token under a false $MSFT ticker claim, with a "500,000 likes and we bring Clippy back" hook. A Microsoft spokesperson confirmed the unauthorized access. The company's apology post read as confusion — and was deleted minutes later.

The nostalgia lesson: the scam worked because it looked like a brand joke — "bring back Clippy" is exactly the kind of thing the real Microsoft might post. Scams that look like jokes bypass everyone's skepticism filter. That's why your people clicked.

Your shop's version of this

  • Your Facebook page, your Google Business profile, your LinkedIn company page. One reused password, one phished session, and your brand is the megaphone. The mechanics are identical at every scale.
  • The verify-on-a-second-channel rule. One post — even from a verified 13-million-follower account — is never enough to act on. Check the company's actual site, or a second channel you trust, before anyone clicks, buys, or shares.
  • The awkward epilogue is the lesson. Microsoft's apology post read as confusion and vanished within minutes. When your brand has its bad day, your crisis comms need to be the calm one — written before you need it, not during.

Why your shop should care — the honest version

This is the funnel's first brand-social beat — a slight lane expansion — so it has to earn its place: it sells the social-engineering lesson, not crypto commentary. Nobody reading this page needs a $Clippy token explainer. They need the transferable part: trusted accounts get hijacked, the scam rides the trust, and the fix is account lockdown plus a verification habit.

It's also the most relatable beat in weeks — everyone saw the Clippy screenshots — which is exactly why it travels further than the admin beats.

Five actions — the brand-account lockdown

1. Hardware-backed admin logins on every brand account

Facebook page, Google Business, LinkedIn — if it can post as your company, it gets a security key or device-bound passkey. No exceptions for “it's just social.”

2. Short session lifetimes

Don't stay logged in forever on shared machines. The shorter the session, the smaller the theft window.

3. Alerting on profile and follow changes

If someone swaps your logo or starts following strange accounts, you want to know in minutes, not days.

4. The second-channel rule, written down

One post is never enough to act on — especially one asking for money or clicks. Make it policy, not instinct.

5. Write the calm crisis post now

One paragraph: what happened, what you're doing, where to check for updates. Save it somewhere you'll find it at 11pm. Microsoft had to improvise; you don't.
