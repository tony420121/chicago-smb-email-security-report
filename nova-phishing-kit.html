EvilTokens died to a court order. N0va is already doing the same job.

On September 22, 2026, Microsoft killed EvilTokens — the AI-powered device-code phishing service behind 12,000 hacked inboxes. The court order didn't end device-code phishing. It made room for the next kit. N0va runs the same playbook through Microsoft's own sign-in pages, and one phished session can become broad SSO access.

What N0va is, in plain English

N0va is a phishing kit — a criminal product, sold or rented to attackers — that targets organizations across North America and Europe. Its lures impersonate Microsoft Security, Teams, SharePoint, OneDrive, DocuSign, Google Drive, Dropbox, Zoom, and Adobe Sign: the brands your employees click without thinking.

Here's the trick. N0va doesn't steal your password. It starts an authentication request on Microsoft's real infrastructure and convinces the victim to complete it — entering a device code on the legitimate Microsoft sign-in page. The victim signs in and completes MFA as normal. But the session being authorized belongs to the attacker. The result: valid access and refresh tokens, no password theft required, and your MFA did exactly what it was designed to do — for the wrong person.

Read that again: MFA completed successfully — and the attacker got in. The victim was on Microsoft's real authentication service, approved their own sign-in, and handed the session to a criminal. This is the one attack where "just turn on MFA" is not the answer.

N0va goes further: researchers observed token exchange and device registration, attempts to establish Primary Refresh Token-based single sign-on access. One phished session can become broad access across every app tied to that identity — email, SharePoint, Teams, all of it.

The infrastructure hides in plain sight too: compromised legitimate websites, Cloudflare Workers, and Linode Object Storage. Blocking one bad domain doesn't work when the kit lives on infrastructure you trust.

Why the usual defenses don't catch it

Device-code phishing breaks the advice owners give their staff. "Check the domain before entering your password" — the domain is correct. The victim is on Microsoft's real authentication service. MFA completed successfully. Every individual security tool may show only one fragment of the compromise, which is especially dangerous for smaller shops without a dedicated identity-security team.

This isn't one kit, either. The pattern is now a template: eSentire's threat response unit identified GhostCode, a separate device-code phishing kit, in late August 2026 — same abuse of Microsoft's OAuth device-authorization flow, same token-theft outcome. (Computerworld)

The sequel framing, stated plainly: the EvilTokens takedown worked — the service is dead. But the court order didn't end device-code phishing; it just made room for the next kit. N0va is the next kit. Defend the pattern, not the name.

The three defenses — same as the takedown taught us

1. Block the device-code flow

Device-code authentication exists for devices that can't do a normal sign-in — smart TVs, meeting-room gear. Your staff's laptops are not those devices. In Microsoft Entra (your Microsoft 365 admin center), restrict or disable device-code sign-in for users, unless a specific line-of-business need requires it. Five minutes in admin for most shops.

2. Enforce phishing-resistant MFA

SMS codes and push-notification approvals can be intercepted or fatigued into submission. Move staff to passkeys, FIDO2 security keys, or number-matching authenticator approval — methods where the attacker can't forward a code and win. Start with email and finance users.

3. Revoke sessions — not just passwords

When an account is compromised, changing the password does not log out the attacker's stolen session. Session tokens survive password resets. After any suspected compromise: revoke the user's active sessions in Entra/365 admin and reset the password — in that order. And hunt your sign-in logs for the device-code grant type paired with unfamiliar client IDs or device-registration events right after sign-in.

Owner action checklist — this week

  • Restrict the device-code flow in Entra — unless you can name a device that needs it.
  • Move email and finance staff to number-matching authenticator approval or passkeys.
  • Write down your incident step zero: revoke sessions first, reset password second.
  • Review active sessions and device registrations for any account that completed a device-code sign-in from an unfamiliar app or location.
  • Tell staff the new rule: if anyone — even someone claiming to be IT — asks you to enter a code from an email or screen into a Microsoft login page, stop and verify through a channel you trust.

The uncomfortable connection: device-code phishing gets attackers in the door. Missing email authentication hands them your letterhead — the report's scan found 81.5% of Chicago SMB domains lack DMARC enforcement, meaning once an attacker holds a session token on an employee's inbox, they can send mail as your business and most receivers will deliver it. Close both doors.
