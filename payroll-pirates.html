They're not after your password — they're after your payroll.

Microsoft tracks an active campaign it calls Payroll Pirates (Storm-2755): stealing Microsoft 365 authenticated sessions and aiming them at payroll and finance mailboxes. Live right now. The payoff isn't your data — it's a wire transfer.

The banking-change scam

Picture it: a "vendor" emails your bookkeeper new wire instructions — from your owner's own compromised mailbox. Nothing looks phished, because nothing was phished. The attacker is sitting inside the session, watching the money move, waiting for the right invoice. Microsoft tracks this as Payroll Pirates (Storm-2755): an adversary-in-the-middle operation stealing Microsoft 365 authenticated sessions — not passwords, sessions — aimed at payroll and finance mailboxes: payment workflows, approvals, banking-change traffic. Across the US, Canada, and Europe.

Why your MFA didn't stop it: adversary-in-the-middle steals the authenticated session after the login succeeds — the text code, the tap, the push approval all happened legitimately. Texts and taps don't stop session theft.

Three checks that catch it

  • Mailbox forwarding and delegate rules on every finance and payroll mailbox — the classic quiet persistence. The rule that silently copies everything to an outside address is the oldest trick in this campaign's book.
  • The "Original transfer method" field in sign-in logs — it shows how the session really authenticated. Look for anything that doesn't match how your people actually sign in.
  • Unexpected device-code authentication prompts. If nobody started a device-code flow, someone else did — treat it as an incident.

Four actions — the payroll four

1. Check forwarding and delegate rules

On every payroll and finance mailbox. Today. The quiet rule forwarding copies of everything to an outside address is the oldest trick in this campaign's book.

2. Check the “Original transfer method” field

In your sign-in logs for those mailboxes. It tells you how the session actually authenticated — look for anything that doesn't match how your people really sign in.

3. Treat unexpected device-code prompts as an incident

If nobody started a device-code flow, someone else did.

4. Move finance staff to device-bound passkeys or security keys

Texts and taps don't stop session theft. This is the fix; the checks above are the detection.
