One attacker, four ransomware brands, the same playbook.

Microsoft named Storm-2570: a single ransomware affiliate, tracked since April 2025, deploying Qilin, DragonForce, Anubis, and BERT. The brand on the ransom note changes. The playbook behind it doesn't.

The playbook, step by step

Microsoft's point is that defenders keep classifying incidents by the ransomware payload — the brand name on the note — and miss the recurring human operator underneath. Storm-2570 is, in effect, a freelance intruder renting whichever ransomware brand pays best on a given job. Victims span the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Spain, the Netherlands, and Puerto Rico — hospitals, schools, government offices, energy firms, retailers, manufacturers. The through-line isn't the malware; it's the tradecraft.

The post-compromise playbook, consistent across all four brands:

  • Legitimate remote-management tools, installed and renamed to blend in: Atera, MeshAgent, ScreenConnect, Splashtop, Remotely_Agent, NinjaRMM.
  • Hidden outbound tunnels using Cloudflare's own tunneling software — intruders slipping past firewalls that only inspect incoming traffic.
  • Credential theft, then lateral movement, then security controls disabled.
  • Data exfiltrated before encryption — the double-extortion model: they steal it, then they scramble it.

Why the ransom note tells you nothing about who broke in

Microsoft's defensive argument: track the operator's repeatable behavior and you get detection chances before the encryption chapter — remote access, credential theft, lateral movement, security tampering, and data exfiltration all leave signals first.

The honest framing: these are your MSP's tools. Atera, ScreenConnect, NinjaRMM — these are the same legitimate tools managed service providers use to support small shops. That's exactly why the affiliate uses them: they blend in. The point is not to fear the tools; it's to know which tools are supposed to be on your machines, so the one that isn't supposed to be there stands out.

Three places to catch it before the encryption chapter

1. Unapproved remote-management software on endpoints

If a remote-access tool appears that your MSP didn't install, that's the signal — not the ransom note three steps later.

2. Unexpected outbound tunnels

Cloudflared or similar tunneling traffic your network didn't authorize is an intruder building a back door your firewall can't see.

3. Credential-theft telemetry

Unusual access to credential stores or mass authentication attempts precede the lateral movement that precedes the encryption.

Five actions — the owner checklist

1. Get the written tool list from your IT person or MSP

Every remote-access tool installed on your machines, in writing. If they can't produce it, that's finding #1.

2. Question anything unrecognized

Especially tools with renamed executables. "What's this one for?" is a complete security audit at small-shop scale.

3. Confirm tamper protection is on

Your endpoint security software must be set so an intruder can't quietly disable it. Security tampering is a named step in this playbook.

4. Make your backups unreachable

Immutable or offline — because this playbook exfiltrates then encrypts.

5. Shorten the admin-credential list

Review who holds admin credentials and where they're stored. Credential theft is step three of this playbook; make it a short list.
