They sell houses. Ransomware hit them anyway.

This week's alleged victim wasn't a bank or a hospital — it was a small real-estate agency. Crews don't pick victims by revenue. They pick by: reachable, holding monetizable records, and easy.

Why a real-estate office

The alleged victim this week was a small real-estate agency — a shop that sells houses. The ransomware crew that allegedly hit them didn't care about the industry. They cared about what the agency holds: customer IDs and financial documents, property records, business communications — exactly the material that monetizes. If your business keeps customer records, money paperwork, and email, you hold the same inventory.

Alleged means alleged: this week's claim is unverified — no confirmed breach, no confirmed data theft. This page treats it as a reported claim that matches the pattern. The pattern is the point, not the proof.

The three things that made them an easy mark

  • One shared login everybody uses — a password nobody owns is a password everybody leaks.
  • No MFA on the accounts that matter, starting with email.
  • No offline backups — so when the files encrypted, there was nothing to restore from.

Three actions — the three cheapest fixes

1. Kill the shared login

One login per person, starting with email. A shared password is a password nobody owns — and everybody leaks.

2. MFA on everything, email first

The account the crew wants most is the mailbox. Authenticator app or security key; texts are better than nothing.

3. Backups the ransomware can't reach

Offline or immutable copies, tested with an actual restore. A backup you've never restored is a rumor.
