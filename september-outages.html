Twice in one month: Microsoft 365 stopped working in September 2026.

Your login and your phones, inside one month. Here's what actually broke on September 16 and September 21 — and the five-item plan for the next time it happens.

What broke on September 16

Single sign-on started failing around 1:22 PM UTC, hitting users in the United States and United Kingdom hardest. People saw 502 and 503 server errors instead of their email, and the Microsoft 365 and Azure admin portals went intermittently unavailable too — which meant admins couldn't even open the Service health dashboard to see what was wrong. By about 6 PM UTC, Microsoft said impact reports had largely subsided and telemetry looked stable, while noting its investigation hadn't found a corresponding issue inside the Microsoft 365 service environment.

What broke on September 21

Five days later, Microsoft disclosed a Teams Calling service incident — incident reference TM1475580 in the Microsoft 365 admin center — in which "some users" couldn't make or receive calls within Microsoft Teams. Microsoft published no affected regions, no root cause, and no restoration timeline. Chat and meetings were a separate question; the confirmed break was voice.

Honest about what the coverage actually established: this page reports what Microsoft and the tech press confirmed at the time. Microsoft published no root cause for the September 16 sign-in failure and no root cause or timeline for the September 21 Teams Calling incident. Anything beyond that is speculation — and this page won't do speculation.

Why "twice in one month" is the story, not the details

Neither incident, on its own, is the end of the world. The pattern is the point. When the cloud goes down, it hits different from a server dying in your back room: your login, your email, your phones, and your admin dashboard all live behind the same sign-in door. September 16 proved the admin dashboard can go down with everything else — so "I'll just check the status page" isn't a plan unless you've set up the alerts somewhere you can reach them when M365 is dark.

The five-item plan for the next one

1. Set up a backup comms channel now

A group text thread, a personal phone number list — something that works when Teams and Outlook don't. Test it once.

2. Turn on Service health alerts

In the Microsoft 365 admin center, find the Service health section and set up email (and ideally SMS/phone) notifications for incidents. Don't rely on opening the dashboard during an outage — September 16 showed the dashboard can be down too.

3. Check cached credentials

Confirm key machines can still log in offline (Windows cached credentials / PIN) so a sign-in outage doesn't strand every laptop.

4. Keep a who-can-still-sign-in roster

Know which accounts don't depend on cloud SSO — your break-glass admin account, any on-prem systems — and where their passwords live.

5. Claim your service credits

Microsoft's SLAs include service-credit terms for qualifying outages. After an incident, ask your Microsoft reseller or check your agreement — most small shops never claim what's owed.
