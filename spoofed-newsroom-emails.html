9,000 spoofed emails wearing a newsroom’s name.

A Japanese news organization disclosed this week that attackers had unauthorized access to its Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace since late July — then sent roughly 9,000 spoofed emails wearing its name on September 30, 2026. Personal information of 1,646 people may have been exposed. The lesson isn't about newsrooms.

What happened — the numbers, stated exactly

  • Late July 2026: attackers gained unauthorized access to the organization's Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace.
  • Early August 2026: Google flagged the suspicious activity to the organization.
  • September 30, 2026: roughly 9,000 spoofed emails sent under the organization's name.
  • 1,646 people — employees and business partners — had personal information potentially exposed, and were warned to expect more impersonation emails.

Attribution note: this is the organization's own announcement, as summarized by finance.biggo.com. We're citing their numbers exactly — not the aggregator's framing.

Spoofed from you vs. spoofed in you

Two different disasters. Spoofed-from-you: attackers send mail that looks like it comes from your domain, to your customers and partners. Your name does the conning. Spoofed-in-you: attackers get inside your mail system — like they did here for two months — and send from the real thing. This incident was the second kind, which is worse: no spam filter on earth flags mail from the actual account.

The lesson, stated carefully: this was not a small business — and that's the point. If attackers can live inside a newsroom's mail for two months, your domain is easier, not harder. You have fewer people watching, fewer alerts configured, and the same trusted name your customers believe.

The honest version

Brand damage is the math nobody does until it's their name: 1,646 people got a letter saying "emails in our name may be scams." Every one of them now hesitates before opening real mail from that organization. For a Chicago shop, your domain is your reputation with customers — and unlike the newsroom, you don't have a comms team to send the apology. The three checks below are the cheapest brand insurance you can buy.

And the compliance sibling: email authentication isn't just best practice anymore — an SMB standard now requires it. The standard is catching up to the attacks.

Three checks — your domain's anti-spoofing kit

1. DMARC, SPF, and DKIM — on your own domain, set correctly

These three records tell the world's mail servers which senders are really you. Without them, anyone can wear your domain like a costume. With them (and DMARC set to actually reject), the costume stops working. Run the free checker below — it shows your red/yellow/green in seconds.

2. Watch for the inside job, not just the costume

This attack lived inside the mail system for two months. Review who has mailbox access, turn on login alerts for impossible travel and new devices, and treat any "weird sent mail" report from staff as urgent until proven otherwise.

3. Make "verify on a second channel" a house rule

Any email asking for money, credentials, or a changed wire destination gets confirmed by phone or in person — every time, no exceptions. The newsroom warned 1,646 people to expect more impersonation emails. Your rule should exist before you have to send that warning.
