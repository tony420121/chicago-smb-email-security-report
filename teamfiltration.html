Attackers didn't break in through your people — they walked in through accounts nobody owns.

On September 22, 2026, Proofpoint disclosed a campaign that probed 5,714 Microsoft 365 accounts across 28 tenants. The seven accounts they actually broke into had one thing in common: every one was a forgotten service account with a default or never-rotated password and no MFA. Not one belonged to a person. Your tenant has accounts like these. Here's how to find them first.

What happened

Proofpoint's report, "Spraying in the Andes: TeamFiltration Returns to Exploit Forgotten Service Accounts," published September 22, 2026 and widely covered on September 24, describes a campaign — tracked as UNK_CondorFiltration — that:

5,714
unique accounts targeted across 28 Microsoft 365 tenants
1,487
unique AWS EC2 source IP addresses
7
accounts confirmed compromised — all unmanaged service accounts
  • Ran in three waves from late July through August 2026, focused on Chilean retail and financial institutions (one Chilean retailer took 78.3% of all observed authentication events)
  • Generated 32,825 authentication events against those 5,714 accounts
  • Compromised 7 accounts — all unmanaged functional or service accounts, not individual employee accounts; every one carried default or unrotated passwords with no multi-factor authentication
  • Broke into six of the seven within 7 minutes, suggesting a shared or default password set rather than individual targeting
  • Pivoted fast: within about two minutes of compromise, the operator moved to a German VPN node to probe the corporate VPN, access the Azure Portal, browse SharePoint Online, and request Microsoft Graph API tokens

Sources: Proofpoint Threat Insight, Sept 22, 2026 — "Spraying in the Andes: TeamFiltration Returns to Exploit Forgotten Service Accounts" — proofpoint.com · The Hacker News, Sept 24, 2026 — thehackernews.com

The tool: TeamFiltration

TeamFiltration is a legitimate, cross-platform offensive security framework — built for penetration testers to enumerate Entra ID accounts, spray passwords, exfiltrate data, and plant OneDrive backdoors. Criminals now use the same tool. That's the pattern running through every attack story this year: the attackers stopped bringing malware and started bringing your IT department's toolbox.

How to spot it in your sign-in logs

Proofpoint published indicators of compromise with the report. Two you can check today:

1. The hardcoded user agent. TeamFiltration's default configuration sends this user-agent string: Teams/1.3.00.30866 (inside a longer Mozilla/Chrome/Electron string). Proofpoint's words: "Not seen in legitimate modern Teams clients." Search your Entra sign-in logs for it — it shouldn't be there.

2. The spray infrastructure. The campaign's source IP ranges: 3.101.0.0/16, 18.144.76.0/24, 13.52.201.0/24 — all AWS EC2. A burst of authentication attempts from these ranges is worth a look. (Post-access, the operator used a German VPN node at 149.88.104.19 to probe VPN, Azure Portal, and SharePoint.)

The honest scope note

5,700 is the number of accounts targeted, not breached — seven were confirmed compromised. And Proofpoint stresses that sign-in telemetry alone can't prove data was actually exfiltrated. Don't let anyone sell you the scarier version of this story. The real lesson is smaller and worse: the doors that opened were accounts nobody remembered creating, with passwords nobody ever changed.

The service-account audit — do this

1. List every account that isn't a person

In the Microsoft Entra admin center, look at your user list — filter for accounts with no recent sign-in, or ask your IT person to export the list. Service accounts, functional accounts, "scanner@," "backup@," vendor accounts.

2. Give each one an owner — or kill it

Every account gets a named human responsible for it. No owner, no reason to exist: disable it.

3. Rotate every password you find

If the password is the vendor's default or older than your youngest employee's tenure, change it today.

4. Put MFA on all of them

No exceptions — Proofpoint's telemetry showed an MFA-enrollment event after one account was accessed, meaning MFA wasn't configured when the attacker walked in.

5. Check what each account can touch

A service account that only needs one app shouldn't have admin roles. Shrink the permissions to what it actually uses.

6. Search your sign-in logs for the user agent above

If Teams/1.3.00.30866 shows up, treat it as an incident, not a curiosity.
